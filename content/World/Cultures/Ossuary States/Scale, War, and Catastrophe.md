# Scale, War, and Catastrophe

## The unknown upper limit

A handful of bones can cultivate a child. No one knows what hundreds or thousands of bones can produce. Entire civilizations have disappeared attempting to answer that question.

Old battlefields are feared and mass graves are carefully managed because bones are fertile. Leaving them unattended is profoundly irresponsible—not because reverence for the dead demands it, but because an unmanaged biological process may continue growing.

## Military cultivation

The military cultivates on a large scale. Recruits periodically donate bones during training, and entire generations of soldiers may eventually emerge from communal nurseries.

Whether this is noble service or exploitation remains an active philosophical debate within skeleton society. Their biology makes the distinction difficult: donation may be voluntary in the moment while the institution benefits from bodies, aptitudes, and descendants across generations.

## What the skeletons fear

Skeletons do not primarily fear death. They fear scale.

Every natural and social process may have an upper limit:

- Cultivation;
- bodily pattern or “memory”;
- cities;
- belief;
- war.

No one knows where those limits lie. Many of history's greatest disasters began when someone asked, **“What happens if we keep going?”**

The civilization's patience is therefore learned survival, not timidity. Battlefields, experimental nurseries, and government laboratories are reminders that stewardship can fail without becoming simple villainy.
