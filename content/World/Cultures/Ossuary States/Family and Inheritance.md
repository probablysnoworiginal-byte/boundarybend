# Family and Inheritance

## Children are cultivated

Children are not born. A family spends twenty years tending the nursery in which its child develops, making the nursery as much a home as the household itself.

Multiple parents are ordinary. Contributions may come from:

- lovers;
- friends;
- mentors;
- siblings;
- larger communities; or
- any combination willing to share responsibility.

Family is defined by responsibility rather than blood. Donation begins the process, but sustained care makes the family.

## Literal inheritance

When an individual's skull is destroyed, every other bone may be reused. Because bones retain physical habits and patterns, a child, artisan, or soldier may carry a bone once belonging to someone remarkable.

This is not the transfer of the dead person's memories or identity. It is inherited bodily aptitude: a hand accustomed to a craft, an arm accustomed to a weapon, or some other persistent pattern.

The cultural and legal boundaries of this reuse—consent, ownership, obligation, prestige, and the risk of coercion—remain to be developed.

## Care as kinship

The twenty-year cultivation period makes parenthood an extended act of stewardship before the child can reciprocate or even emerge. It fits the broader Ossuary conviction that people do not control old natural processes; they accept responsibility for tending them. See [[Stewardship and Material Culture]].
